# LVMH 2025: Revenue Stabilizes, But Profit Drops 13% as Luxury Slowdown Deepens

LVMH, the world's largest luxury goods group, reported €80.8 billion in revenue for 2025, down 1% on an organic basis. The company showed resilience in a difficult year, with organic growth returning in the second half after a weak first half. But profit from recurring operations fell 9% to €17.8 billion, and net profit dropped 13% to €10.9 billion. The Fashion & Leather Goods division — home to Louis Vuitton, Dior, and Fendi — saw revenue decline 5% organically, though it maintained a 35% operating margin.

Here's what happened.

## The Numbers: Revenue Stabilizes, Profit Declines

- **Revenue:** €80.8 billion, down 5% reported, down 1% organic
- **Profit from recurring operations:** €17.8 billion, down 9%
- **Operating margin:** 22%
- **Net profit (Group share):** €10.9 billion, down 13%
- **Operating free cash flow:** €11.3 billion, up 8%

*Translation: "Organic" revenue growth strips out the impact of currency fluctuations and acquisitions/divestitures, showing the underlying business performance. LVMH's reported revenue fell 5%, but 3 percentage points of that decline came from unfavorable currency movements (mainly a weaker yen and weaker Chinese yuan). The organic decline of 1% is the cleaner number to focus on — it tells you the business was essentially flat in 2025.*

The fourth quarter showed organic revenue growth of 1%, in line with the third quarter. That's a meaningful improvement from the first half of 2025, when LVMH was in decline. The company is stabilizing, but it's not growing yet.

*Translation: Luxury goods companies typically report "organic growth" on a quarterly basis to show momentum. LVMH returning to positive organic growth in Q3 and Q4 (both +1%) after negative growth in H1 is a sign the worst may be over. But +1% growth is barely above zero — this is stabilization, not a recovery.*

## Fashion & Leather Goods: The Profit Engine Slows

Fashion & Leather Goods — LVMH's largest and most profitable division — generated €37.8 billion in revenue, down 5% organically. This segment includes Louis Vuitton, Dior, Fendi, Celine, Loewe, and other iconic brands.

Despite the revenue decline, the division maintained an operating margin of 35%, down from 37% in 2024. Profit from recurring operations fell 13% to €13.2 billion.

*Translation: A 35% operating margin means Fashion & Leather Goods keeps €0.35 of every €1 it earns. That's extraordinarily high — most retailers operate at 5-15% margins. The fact that LVMH can maintain a 35% margin even during a slowdown shows the pricing power of brands like Louis Vuitton and Dior. But the 2-percentage-point drop (from 37% to 35%) tells you the company is either spending more on marketing and stores, or having to offer more discounts to move product.*

LVMH said the division benefited from "solid local demand" — meaning wealthy customers in their home markets (especially the US and Europe) are still buying, even as Chinese tourists and aspirational buyers pull back.

## Selective Retailing: Sephora Keeps Growing

Selective Retailing (primarily Sephora and DFS duty-free stores) generated €18.3 billion in revenue, up 4% organically. This was LVMH's best-performing division in 2025.

Sephora continued to grow revenue and profit, consolidating its position as the world leader in beauty retail. Profit from recurring operations for the division rose 4% to €2.2 billion.

*Translation: Sephora is LVMH's hidden gem. While the luxury fashion brands struggle with slowing demand from ultra-wealthy customers, Sephora sells to a much broader customer base. Beauty products are more affordable and more frequently purchased than a €3,000 handbag, which makes Sephora more resilient during economic slowdowns. The fact that Sephora is still growing while Fashion & Leather Goods declines shows LVMH's diversification is paying off.*

## Watches & Jewelry: Tiffany Stabilizes

Watches & Jewelry (Tiffany, Bulgari, TAG Heuer, Hublot) generated €10.5 billion in revenue, up 3% organically. Profit from recurring operations rose 7% to €1.8 billion.

LVMH highlighted the success of Tiffany's renovated stores in Milan and Tokyo, and the strong performance of "iconic lines" across its watch and jewelry brands.

*Translation: Tiffany has been a turnaround story for LVMH since the company acquired it in 2021 for $15.8 billion. The brand was struggling before the acquisition, but LVMH has invested heavily in renovating stores and refreshing product lines. The fact that Watches & Jewelry is now growing 3% organically — faster than the group average — suggests the Tiffany turnaround is working.*

## Perfumes & Cosmetics: Flat Growth

Perfumes & Cosmetics (Dior, Givenchy, Guerlain, Benefit) generated €8.2 billion in revenue, flat organically. Profit from recurring operations rose 8% to €727 million.

LVMH said the division benefited from "successful innovations and highly selective retail approach." The company is focusing on high-margin products and premium distribution channels rather than chasing volume.

## Wines & Spirits: The Biggest Decline

Wines & Spirits (Moët & Chandon, Dom Pérignon, Hennessy cognac) generated €5.4 billion in revenue, down 5% organically. Profit from recurring operations plunged 25% to €1.0 billion.

LVMH said champagne and wine revenue was stable, but cognac demand was "weaker." Hennessy cognac has been hit particularly hard by slowing demand in China and the US.

*Translation: Cognac is a luxury spirit that's heavily dependent on Chinese demand. When China's economy slows and consumer confidence drops, cognac sales fall sharply. Hennessy is the world's largest cognac brand, so LVMH is particularly exposed. The 25% profit decline (on only a 5% revenue decline) tells you the company is either cutting prices to move inventory, or absorbing higher costs without being able to pass them on to customers.*

## Geographic Performance: Asia Stabilizes, Europe Weakens

LVMH's geographic performance in 2025 was mixed:

**Europe:** Declined in the second half of 2025. Local demand weakened as economic uncertainty increased.

**United States:** Grew, benefiting from solid local demand. Wealthy Americans continued to buy luxury goods despite broader economic concerns.

**Japan:** Declined compared to 2024. The prior year had been boosted by a surge in tourist spending due to the weak yen. In 2025, the yen strengthened and tourist spending normalized.

**Rest of Asia:** Showed "noticeable improvement in trends" compared to 2024, with a return to growth in the second half. This is code for: China stabilized.

*Translation: LVMH is heavily dependent on Chinese consumers, both in mainland China and as tourists shopping abroad. When LVMH says "the rest of Asia saw a noticeable improvement," they're really saying Chinese demand stopped falling. It's not growing strongly yet, but it's no longer in freefall. That's a positive sign, but the bar is low.*

## The Bottom Line for Investors

LVMH is navigating a difficult environment. The company returned to organic growth in the second half of 2025, but at +1%, it's barely growing. Profit margins are compressing as the company spends to maintain brand desirability (new stores, marketing, events) while demand softens.

The bull case is that LVMH owns the world's most powerful luxury brands, and wealthy consumers will always want Louis Vuitton, Dior, and Tiffany. The company is investing heavily in experiences (The Louis in Shanghai, renovated Tiffany stores, Formula 1 partnership) to maintain brand heat. If the global economy stabilizes and Chinese demand recovers, LVMH's pricing power and operating leverage will drive profit growth. The 8% increase in free cash flow (to €11.3 billion) shows the business is still generating cash even in a down year.

The bear case is that luxury demand is structurally slowing, not just cyclically weak. Aspirational buyers (the middle class in China and elsewhere) are pulling back, and even ultra-wealthy customers are becoming more selective. Fashion & Leather Goods — LVMH's profit engine — saw revenue decline 5% and operating margin compress from 37% to 35%. If this trend continues, LVMH's valuation premium (the company trades at a significant premium to other luxury goods companies) may no longer be justified. And the 13% drop in net profit shows that even with aggressive cost management, LVMH can't fully offset revenue declines.

The question is whether 2025 was the bottom, or the beginning of a longer slowdown.
