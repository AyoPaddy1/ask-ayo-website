# Disney Q1 FY2026: Parks Hit Record Revenue, Streaming Profitable, CEO Succession Looms

Disney beat Wall Street expectations with $26.0 billion in revenue (up 5%) and $1.63 adjusted earnings per share. The Experiences division (theme parks, cruises, resorts) posted record quarterly revenue of $10.0 billion. Streaming (Disney+, Hulu) turned a profit with an 8.4% operating margin. But total segment operating income fell 9% to $4.6 billion, as higher content costs in Entertainment and Sports offset the gains from Parks and streaming.

Here's what happened.

## The Numbers: Revenue Beat, But Profits Declined

- **Revenue:** $26.0 billion, up 5% (expected $25.6 billion)
- **Adjusted EPS:** $1.63 (expected $1.57-1.58)
- **Total segment operating income:** $4.6 billion, down 9%
- **GAAP EPS:** $1.34, down from $1.40 last year

*Translation: Disney beat expectations on the top line (revenue) and bottom line (earnings per share), but the quality of the earnings declined. Total segment operating income — the profit generated by Disney's three main businesses (Entertainment, Sports, Experiences) — fell 9%. That's a red flag. It means Disney is spending more to generate each dollar of revenue than it did a year ago.*

The adjusted EPS of $1.63 strips out one-time charges and accounting adjustments to show the underlying business performance. This is the number Wall Street focuses on, and Disney beat the $1.57-1.58 consensus estimate.

## Experiences: The Profit Engine Keeps Running

Disney's Experiences division — which includes Disneyland, Walt Disney World, Disney Cruise Line, and international parks — posted record quarterly revenue of $10.0 billion and operating income of $3.3 billion.

Domestic parks attendance was up 1%, and per capita spending (how much each guest spends on tickets, food, merchandise) was up 4%. Domestic parks operating income grew 8%.

*Translation: Disney's theme parks are still printing money. Even though attendance barely grew (up 1%), Disney squeezed more revenue out of each guest by raising prices on tickets, food, and merchandise. This is classic Disney pricing power — people will pay whatever it costs to take their kids to see Mickey Mouse.*

The 8% operating income growth at domestic parks is strong, especially in an environment where consumer spending is slowing. It shows that Disney's parks business is resilient, even during economic uncertainty.

## Streaming: Finally Profitable

Disney's streaming business (Disney+, Hulu, and Disney+ Hotstar) generated $450 million in operating income on an 8.4% operating margin. That's up $189 million from a year ago.

Streaming revenue grew 11% year-over-year, driven by subscriber growth and higher pricing. Disney is targeting a 10% streaming operating margin for the full fiscal year.

*Translation: For years, Disney lost billions of dollars building its streaming business to compete with Netflix. Now, streaming is finally profitable. An 8.4% operating margin means Disney keeps $0.084 of every dollar it earns from streaming subscriptions. That's not great (Netflix's margin is over 20%), but it's a huge improvement from losing money.*

Disney stopped reporting subscriber numbers this quarter, which suggests the company wants investors to focus on profitability rather than growth. This is a sign that Disney believes the streaming wars are over, and it's time to harvest profits rather than chase market share.

*Translation: When a company stops disclosing a metric it used to report, it's usually because that metric is no longer growing or no longer looks good. Disney stopping subscriber reporting suggests subscriber growth has slowed, and the company wants Wall Street to focus on the fact that streaming is now profitable instead.*

## Entertainment: Higher Costs Eat Into Profits

Disney's Entertainment segment (which includes streaming, linear TV networks like ABC and FX, and theatrical releases) generated $1.1 billion in operating income, down $0.6 billion from a year ago. Operating margin fell to 9.5%.

Revenue grew 7%, but programming and production costs grew faster. Disney spent heavily on content for Disney+ and Hulu, and also absorbed higher marketing costs for theatrical releases.

*Translation: Disney is making more money (revenue up 7%), but spending even more to make that money (operating income down $0.6 billion). This is the opposite of operating leverage. It means Disney's content costs are rising faster than its ability to monetize that content through subscriptions, advertising, and box office.*

Advertising revenue in the Entertainment segment fell 6%, reflecting a tough comparison to last year (which included political advertising and revenue from Star India, which Disney no longer owns).

## Sports (ESPN): YouTube TV Dispute Cost $110 Million

Disney's Sports segment (primarily ESPN) generated $191 million in operating income, down $56 million from a year ago.

Advertising revenue grew 10%, but higher programming and production costs (mainly sports rights fees) more than offset the gains. Disney also took a $110 million hit from the temporary suspension of ESPN on YouTube TV during a carriage dispute.

*Translation: ESPN's business model is under pressure. Advertising revenue is growing, but sports rights fees (the money ESPN pays the NFL, NBA, etc. to broadcast games) are growing faster. This is a structural problem for ESPN — sports leagues keep demanding more money, but ESPN can't raise prices on cable subscribers fast enough to keep up.*

The YouTube TV carriage dispute is a reminder that ESPN's distribution is fragile. If YouTube TV (or any other major distributor) drops ESPN, it costs Disney real money.

## The Bottom Line for Investors

Disney delivered a solid quarter on the surface — revenue and earnings beat expectations, Parks hit a record, and streaming turned profitable. But the details reveal cracks in the foundation. Total segment operating income fell 9%, driven by rising content costs in Entertainment and higher sports rights fees at ESPN.

The bull case is that Disney is investing for the future. The company is spending heavily on content for Disney+ and Hulu to compete with Netflix, and those investments will pay off as streaming margins expand. Parks remain a cash machine, and Disney is doubling its stock buyback to $7 billion, which signals confidence in the business. The company is guiding for double-digit adjusted EPS growth in fiscal 2026, weighted to the second half of the year.

The bear case is that Disney's profit margins are compressing, and the company is spending more to stand still. Entertainment operating income fell despite 7% revenue growth, which means content costs are rising faster than Disney's ability to monetize that content. ESPN is structurally challenged, as sports rights fees keep rising while the cable bundle shrinks. And the fact that Disney stopped reporting streaming subscriber numbers suggests growth has stalled. The CEO succession question also looms — Bob Iger is expected to step down in 2026, and the market is nervous about who will replace him.

The question is whether Disney's investments in content and streaming will pay off, or whether the company is trapped in a cycle of rising costs and shrinking margins.
